The best starting point is often a small operational irritation: the same information copied twice, enquiries waiting for routing or a team repeatedly turning unstructured messages into a standard record. First make that work visible. Then decide which parts need interpretation and which can follow an ordinary rule.

The examples here are illustrative workflow designs. They are not reports of deployed ElevateX systems or measured customer savings.

## Start with the workflow, its owner and its exceptions

Describe the process as it works today. What starts it? Where does the information arrive? Who checks it? Which system is authoritative? What does “finished” mean?

A useful candidate has recurring inputs, an identifiable output and someone responsible when it fails. A process that changes every week or depends on undocumented judgement may first need clarification. Automating an unclear process can make its mistakes repeat more consistently.

Observe several normal cases and several awkward ones. A lead-routing process might encounter a missing email address, an existing customer, an attachment nobody can read or a request covering two services. Those are part of the workload, rather than optional details for a later phase.

Choose a narrow first version with a way to pause it. “Prepare a draft enquiry record for review” is a manageable scope. “Handle sales automatically” leaves too many decisions undefined.

## Separate AI interpretation from ordinary rules

AI is useful when the input involves variable language. It can propose a category for a message, extract candidate details from a document or draft a summary for a person to check.

Known conditions usually belong in conventional code or workflow rules. Checking that a required field exists, looking up a customer ID, enforcing an access permission or choosing a recipient from an approved list does not inherently need a language model.

This separation makes the system easier to inspect. The model proposes information; the application decides which values are valid and which actions are permitted. A plausible sentence is not evidence that a customer exists, a payment succeeded or an appointment is available.

Use an AI step only when it improves the specific job enough to justify its cost, uncertainty and maintenance. A structured form plus a simple routing rule may solve the problem without AI.

## A practical inquiry-routing example

Imagine a service company receiving project enquiries through a working, consented collection channel. The proposed workflow could be:

1. **Collect the original request.** Assign a stable record ID and retain the source needed for review under an appropriate retention policy.
2. **Propose a summary and service category.** An AI step extracts only the fields the team needs. It does not send a quotation or promise a deadline.
3. **Validate the proposal.** Check required values, allowed categories and whether an existing record already represents this enquiry.
4. **Create or update the right record.** A controlled integration writes the approved fields to the CRM and records the outcome.
5. **Notify the responsible person.** Unclear requests and failed validations enter a review queue with enough context to resolve them.

## Where routine work can become simpler

**Lead capture and routing:** propose a service label and send a complete record to the right team. Preserve an “unclear” route so a forced category does not quietly misdirect the enquiry.

**Information extraction:** prepare candidate fields from an incoming document, then validate them against the original. Different layouts, missing pages and ambiguous names need explicit handling.

**Moving information between systems:** copy validated data through an API rather than repeated manual entry. An AI step may be unnecessary if the source already has structured fields.

**Notifications and follow-ups:** trigger a known message after a confirmed event. A reminder based on an actual appointment record is different from a model guessing that an appointment exists.

**Internal summaries:** prepare a draft that helps a team member review a long request. Keep the original accessible, and do not treat the summary as a complete substitute when details affect the decision.

These are opportunity areas, not universal promises. The amount of useful work depends on the real inputs and the quality of the surrounding process.

## Keep human approval where consequences justify it

A workflow can prepare information without receiving permission to make every decision. Pricing exceptions, refunds, contractual commitments, account access changes and ambiguous customer complaints deserve an explicit approval policy.

Microsoft's [Power Automate approvals documentation](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-automate/get-started-approvals) shows one concrete implementation pattern: a flow can request approval and wait for a person's response before continuing. This demonstrates a design option; it does not mean every platform provides the same behaviour or that a particular licence is suitable for your business.

Define who can approve, what evidence they see and what happens when nobody responds. The review queue needs an owner and an overdue path. An unattended approval step can become a new bottleneck.

## Design for retries, duplicates and partial failure

A workflow can fail after one action succeeds. For example, the CRM may accept a new record while the notification service times out. Repeating the entire workflow could create a duplicate record.

Use stable event identifiers and record which actions completed. When an integration supports idempotency, use it according to that provider's contract. Stripe's [idempotent request documentation](https://docs.stripe.com/api/idempotent_requests) gives a specific example of retrying an operation without accidentally performing it twice. Its behaviour is provider-specific; do not assume every API handles retries that way.

For a practical first version, specify:

- which failures can be retried and how often;
- how duplicate events are recognised;
- where unresolved work goes after retries end;
- who receives an alert and can replay or correct the item;
- how to pause the workflow without losing pending work.

The goal is visible recovery. A dashboard full of green executions is not enough if a record was written to the wrong customer.

## Treat incoming text as data, not authority

An email, uploaded document or web page can contain instructions that try to redirect a model. OWASP distinguishes direct prompt injection from indirect injection through external content, and recommends controls including output validation, limited permissions and approval for high-risk actions. See the [official OWASP project source](https://github.com/OWASP/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/blob/main/2_0_vulns/LLM01_PromptInjection.md).

For the inquiry example, a sentence inside a customer message must not change the permitted CRM fields or grant access to another customer's records. Keep secrets out of prompts, restrict connected tools and check permissions in the application that performs the action.

Decide what information may leave the business, which provider processes it and how long it is retained. Collecting more data than the workflow needs makes review and governance harder without necessarily improving the result.

## Evaluate the whole workload

Measure a baseline before the pilot: task frequency, time spent, common exceptions and the quality level required. Then compare the new process under similar conditions.

Count the time spent checking AI output, resolving failures, maintaining integrations and answering questions from the team. A useful evaluation asks whether the same acceptable outcome takes less total effort, rather than whether a model can produce a draft quickly.

Start with a review mode where a person checks proposed changes before they happen. Use representative cases, including incomplete and misleading input. Expand automatic actions only when the error consequences, controls and observed behaviour justify it.

## Questions before an automation project

### Does every automation need AI?

No. If the inputs are structured and the rules are known, a conventional integration may be simpler to test and maintain. Use AI when interpretation adds a useful capability.

### Can it run without human review?

Some bounded, low-consequence actions may be suitable after testing. The decision depends on permissions, reversibility, input quality and the cost of a mistake. Keep a route for exceptions even when normal cases proceed automatically.

### How much time will it save?

That needs a baseline and a measured pilot. Task volume, review effort, failure rates and maintenance can change the answer; a generic percentage cannot establish the result for your process.
